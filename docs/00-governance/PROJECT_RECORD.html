1 Founding brief

SandScope began from a single instruction: build a demonstration of Forward-Deployed Engineering and AI Solutions Architecture capability, run to a real production standard, with a $0 infrastructure ceiling and no wasted motion — stated in the founding brief as "unnecessary spend on tokens, unnecessary brainstorming is a sin in this project." Not a portfolio listing built projects. A new system, simulated end to end, judged on whether the engineering underneath it would survive contact with a real reviewer.

The control plane for AI agents that operate production systems. — PRD.md §1, Vision

2 Business case

The business case is unusual in naming its own fiction upfront. SandScope's customers — the platform and SRE teams it's positioned for — are simulated. The engineering underneath them is not.

"SandScope is a demonstration product built to production standards on synthetic data. The engineering is real: the services, the failover, the retrieval, the evaluation, the tests, the pipeline and the deployment all function. The customers are simulated. This is stated plainly at the top of the document, and it is stated on the live site, because the alternative is a reader discovering it themselves and discounting everything else."

— BRD.md §0, Nature of this document

3 Product

Demonstrated through one flagship workload — production incident triage — because that is where an agent's accountability is tested hardest: it must decide, per question, whether it has enough grounded evidence to answer at all.

BandBehaviour
SUFFICIENTanswer, every claim cited
AMBIGUOUSanswer, flagged partial — never silently upgraded
INSUFFICIENTrefuse — no draft is emitted at all

4 Lifecycle model — five lenses

Every sprint is read through five lenses at once, because none of them alone explains what a piece of work actually is. A task can be MVP-stage product, Implementation-phase SDLC, Development-stage PDLC, AIDLC-7 Serving, and Sprint 2, simultaneously — and the label is meant to be legible on its own, without opening the sprint plan.

01

Product stage

Concept → MVP → GA. Where the product itself sits.

02

SDLC phase

Requirements → Design → Implementation → Test → Release. The engineering process.

03

PDLC stage

Discovery → Definition → Development → Delivery. The product-management process.

04

AIDLC (1–8)

Feasibility → data → context → grounding → evaluation → governance → serving → observability. Mapped to NIST AI RMF.

05

Agile cadence

Sprint number, backlog, velocity, retro. The rhythm all four other lenses move inside.

5 Roles & charter

Eleven delivery roles, four stakeholder roles, one rule that holds the whole thing together: a role may not sign off its own work. QA Lead signs off DEV. The Architect signs off DEV on design conformance. The Product Owner signs off anything that changes scope.

8 Budget

The honest figure, not a rounded one: $0 of infrastructure spend, and no team beyond one person plus an AI agent operating under named roles. There is no headcount to report and no dollar figure to invent, so none is given here.

What "$0" means here

Every hosted dependency — Neon, Upstash, Northflank, Vercel, the LLM providers — runs on a free tier. This is a tested requirement, not a claim: test_no_paid_service_in_deploy_manifest fails the build if a paid host appears in a deployment manifest.

Where it already failed once

ADR-0003 placed the agent runtime on a host that later moved behind a paid plan. The $0 constraint held — the runtime was moved rather than the constraint being quietly dropped — but it held only after the gap was found. Recorded as D-017, reviewed in §9.

9 Council retrospective

Built as skills/role-council — a reusable, git-portable skill that runs a cross-functional review over any repo's real history, then run against this project's own. Every reaction below cites a real defect, ADR, or file; none is an invented opinion. Two of nine reviewed artifacts, in full — the rest are in docs/00-governance/COUNCIL_RETROSPECTIVE.md.

D-001 · severity 1

The Sprint 2 gate reported 0% false-answer rate. The real rate was 56.6%.

The refusal gate marked 150 of 265 unanswerable questions as answerable, on a sample where the Sprint 2 gate had reported zero errors.

QA Lead The test set was written by the implementer, on 22 questions, and every one happened to be easy. The same person who builds a gate shouldn't be the only one deciding what counts as a hard question for it.
Business Analyst A 0% error rate at a sprint gate should have triggered a question before it was accepted, not after a defect was found. If the acceptance criteria didn't require a minimum sample size, that's a gap in what I wrote at Sprint 0.
Technical Program Manager This changes how I'd read every other green metric in the project. If a headline number can be 0% wrong on a small sample and 57% wrong on a large one, "passed at the gate" needs a stated sample size next to it from now on.
D-016 · severity 2

Sprints 6 and 7 were worked and shipped without ever being opened.

No planning ceremony, no plan document — a Sprint 5 retrospective had already committed to raising exactly this as an impediment if it happened. It happened anyway.

Scrum Master The team wrote down the exact failure mode in advance and didn't catch it when it occurred. A committed retrospective action that isn't checked at the next retro isn't an action, it's a wish.
Executive Sponsor The part I actually want to know: was the work good despite the missing ceremony? Reading the backfilled reviews, yes. This is a paperwork failure, not a delivery failure — which matters for how seriously to take it, though it's still worth fixing.
